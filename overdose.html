Say it's an overdose. Stay with them and do what the 911 dispatcher tells you.

This is general first aid information, not medical training. Always follow the 911 dispatcher.

What to do in an overdose

What to do

  1. Check the scene is safe for you and them.
  2. Call 911 and say it's an overdose.
  3. If they're breathing, roll them onto their side.
  4. If they're not breathing, start CPR as the 911 dispatcher directs, and use an AED if one is available.
  5. Naloxone (Narcan). If opioids might be involved and you have naloxone, use it as the package directs. Don't delay CPR to do it.
  6. Stay with them until help arrives.
  7. Tell the paramedics what they took, how much and when, if you know.
More info

Keep them from getting too cold or too hot, and tell them help is on the way.

Good Samaritan laws. The CDC says Good Samaritan laws are in place in most states to protect people who are overdosing, and anyone helping them, from criminal penalties. They vary by state. The DEA's Get Smart About Drugs says they generally cover minor offenses like simple possession, not selling or trafficking. Don't let worry stop you calling 911.

Signs of an overdose

They don't need to have every sign. If you're worried, call 911.

More info

Opioids (like heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone or codeine)

  • Not responding
  • Breathing slowly, or only a few times a minute
  • Gasping or gurgling
  • Bluish or grayish skin

Alcohol

  • Confusion, or can't stay awake or be woken
  • Vomiting
  • Seizures
  • Slow or irregular breathing
  • Slow heart rate and clammy skin
  • Very low body temperature, bluish skin or paleness

Stimulants (like meth)

  • Agitation, anxiety or paranoia
  • Fast heart rate and high body temperature
  • Seizures (convulsions)
  • Signs of a stroke or heart attack

GHB

  • Unconsciousness
  • Seizures
  • Greatly slowed breathing and a slow heart rate
  • Vomiting

What not to do

  • Don't leave them alone.
  • Don't assume they'll be fine sleeping it off.
  • Don't rely on cold showers, coffee or walking them around. They don't reverse an alcohol overdose and could make things worse.

Afterward

They still need medical care, even if they wake up. Naloxone's effects often wear off before the opioid's do.

More info

The CDC says that if you give naloxone, stay with them until emergency help arrives, or for at least four hours, to make sure their breathing returns to normal.

For questions about a possible poisoning or medicine mix-up when they're awake and well, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. If they collapse, have a seizure, have trouble breathing or can't be woken, call 911.

For a crisis, including a substance use crisis, call or text 988. For treatment referrals, call SAMHSA's National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357.

Naloxone (Narcan) and first aid classes

The FDA approved Narcan naloxone nasal spray for over-the-counter use, without a prescription. The CDC says naloxone is available over the counter in all 50 states, at places like pharmacies, convenience stores, grocery stores and gas stations.

More info

Naloxone only reverses opioid overdoses, but the CDC says it won't harm someone who is overdosing on other drugs.

A first aid or CPR class teaches you these skills hands-on. The American Red Cross runs classes across the US.
