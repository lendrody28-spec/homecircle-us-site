Urge surfing

Watch an urge crest and fall without obeying it.

An urge is a wave. It gets stronger, peaks, and eases. You do not have to decide your whole life while it is loud.

Name it in one sentence. Notice where it sits in the body. Set a ten-minute wait: change rooms, drink water, or open a meeting.

When it eases, jot down what helped.

Step by step

  1. Name it

    One sentence is enough. You don't have to obey it.

  2. Locate it

    Warm, tight, restless? You are noticing, not fixing.

  3. Wait ten minutes

    Move, water, a person, or a meeting.

Where this comes from

Urge surfing comes from relapse-prevention research. US psychologist Alan Marlatt described it in the 1980s, and it later became part of Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP), which he developed with colleagues including Sarah Bowen.

Why it can help

Urges tend to rise, peak and ease, like a wave. Watching that happen, instead of fighting the urge or obeying it, can make it easier to wait one out. In a 2009 study of university students who smoked, a short urge-surfing instruction did not make urges weaker, but people smoked fewer cigarettes over the next week. In a 2014 trial, people in MBRP reported fewer days of drug use and less heavy drinking a year later than people in usual aftercare. That study tested a longer, guided program, not this page. Results differ from person to person.

Sources

  • Bowen and Marlatt, 2009 study (PubMed)
  • Bowen and others, 2014 trial, JAMA Psychiatry (PubMed)
